THE BOOK

SYNOPSIS

Wanting to save humanity was just the beginning.

SHORT (50 WORDS)

In 2048, a geophysicist finds numbers in Earth's data that shouldn't exist: the planet's magnetic shield is failing. Decades later, a secret Guild launches the Aurora, a spherical ark carrying 82 people to a world 200 light-years away. A hard science fiction saga about what it costs to start again.

LONG

In 2048, a young geophysicist finds numbers in Earth's own data that shouldn't exist. The world doesn't listen. By the time it does, it's too late to fix what's coming. It's not too late to build something else.

For thirty years, scientists, executives and unlikely believers who call themselves the Guild build an interstellar ark in secret. Alder Campbell is not one of them. He is not a scientist, an engineer or a pilot. He becomes the one thing the mission needs most: someone people can trust.

Alder boards the Aurora alone. His wife has chosen to stay on Earth; his two sons will grow old on a planet he will never see again. On the long crossing, a ship built to outrun a dying world must become something no one designed: a society that can raise children, keep secrets, and decide who gets to lead.

Project Aurora 371 is a hard science fiction saga about what it costs to start again.

LONG

Fabijan D. Svetac is a nomad by vocation — a foreigner everywhere he has lived. He fell in love with science fiction as a child and never quite recovered; in school, biology, chemistry and physics held his attention longest, and today he works in software development and systems integration. Project Aurora 371, his first novel, keeps circling back to a question he has lived with all his life: what it means to build a home somewhere you don't come from.

MEDIUM

Fabijan D. Svetac writes hard science fiction about technology, human nature, and the future of civilization. Fascinated by the questions that emerge when technological possibility meets human limitation, his work turns to survival, planetary crisis, and the possibility of a future beyond Earth. Project Aurora 371 is his first novel.

QUESTIONS THE AUTHOR IS HAPPY TO ANSWER

Interviews are done in writing, by email.

Why is the Aurora a sphere?

Almost every starship in science fiction is long and pointed. Mine is a ball, and that wasn't a design choice. It was the engine's. The gravitational generator wraps itself in a field that spreads evenly in every direction, a bubble. Build something long and the bubble has to grow to cover the whole hull: more field, more energy, more waste. So the engineers did the opposite. As one of them puts it: "We don't force the field to adapt to the spacecraft. We make the spacecraft adapt to the field."

Your ship shares a name with Kim Stanley Robinson's Aurora. Is that deliberate?

The name comes from the Roman goddess of the dawn, not from his book. She stands for renewal, hope and new beginnings. But I'm glad of the company. His Aurora is the strongest case I know against leaving. I gave that case to one of my characters and let her keep it.

How is this different from Seveneves?

Stephenson gives humanity two years to save itself. I gave it ninety, which turns out to be harder: there's time to look away. When the ministers in my book hear "a few decades", they relax. A deadline two years away unites people. A deadline ninety years away tests them.

Chapter 2 already tells us they arrive. Why give that away so early?

I wanted the reader to stop asking whether they make it and start asking what it cost and who paid. An old man telling a boy how they got there is a different book from a countdown.

Where's the villain?

There's no villain because I don't think that's where the danger is. The hardest scene in my book is two people who love each other at a kitchen table with a form between them, and both of them are right. I find that more frightening than a saboteur.
